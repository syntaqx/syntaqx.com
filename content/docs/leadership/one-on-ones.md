A 1:1 is the one meeting where the person who reports to you sets the agenda. When it turns into a status update, both people lose it: the manager gets information that a standup or a ticket would have given them, and the report loses the only regular, private time to raise what's actually on their mind.

## Their Meeting, Not Yours

The report owns the agenda. I keep a shared doc per person with a running list; either of us can add to it during the week, and their items come first. When they arrive with nothing, that's a signal too, and a good time for open questions: what's frustrating you, what would you change about how the team works, what are you not getting enough of.

Status doesn't belong here. If a 1:1 is mostly "what did you ship this week", the team is missing a better place for that information, and the conversations that need privacy aren't happening anywhere.

## Cadence

Weekly, 30 minutes, for direct reports. Weekly is often enough that small things get raised while they're small, and regular enough that skipping one is noticeable. Move them when you must, but don't cancel them; a manager who regularly cancels 1:1s is telling the report where they rank.

Start a new relationship by learning how the person works. Lara Hogan's [first 1:1 questions](https://larahogan.me/blog/first-one-on-one-questions/) are a good set: how they like to get feedback, what makes them grumpy and how you'll know, what they want from the next year, and how they like to be recognized. Her advice is to have the answers "WAY before you need them".

## Giving Feedback

Feedback that's vague ("be more proactive"), late (saved for the performance review), or about character ("you're careless") doesn't change anything. The person can't act on it, and often can't even tell what it's about.

The structure I use is the Center for Creative Leadership's [Situation-Behavior-Impact](https://www.ccl.org/articles/leading-effectively-articles/closing-the-gap-between-intent-vs-impact-sbii/):

- **Situation:** when and where. "In Tuesday's design review..."
- **Behavior:** what they did, observably. "...you cut Sam off twice before he finished his proposal..."
- **Impact:** what it caused. "...and he didn't raise his concern about the migration, which turned out to be right."

Then ask about intent, the extra step CCL adds: "what was going on for you there?" The person may have had a reason you didn't see, and the conversation becomes two-way instead of a verdict.

- **Timely.** Within a day or two, while both people remember. Feedback saved up for review time feels like an ambush and arrives too late to help.
- **Positive feedback in the same form.** "Good job" teaches nothing. "The way you wrote up the rollback plan before the migration meant we recovered in ten minutes" tells someone exactly what to keep doing.
- **Private for corrective, public (if they like it) for praise.**

## Receiving Feedback

Ask for it, specifically and often: "what's one thing I could have done better in that planning meeting?" gets an answer; "any feedback for me?" gets "nope, all good". When you get it, thank the person, ask questions to understand it, and resist explaining yourself in the moment. Then visibly act on it. Nothing makes a team stop giving feedback faster than giving it and seeing nothing change.

## Growth Conversations

Every few weeks, set aside a 1:1 for the longer view: where they want to be in a year or two, what they want to learn, what kind of work energizes them. Tie it to the team's [career ladder](/docs/leadership/career-ladders) so "growth" has a shared language, and to actual opportunities: the project they could lead, the design they could own. Growth that only exists in 1:1 notes isn't growth.

## Skip-Levels

Managers of managers should hold skip-level 1:1s with the people two levels down, less often (monthly or quarterly). They're not for going around the manager; they're for hearing what doesn't survive being passed up the chain, and for being a real person to the people your decisions affect.

## Write Things Down

Keep notes in the shared doc: what was raised, what you agreed, who's doing what. A promise made in a 1:1 and forgotten does more damage than no promise at all, and notes make it easy to notice patterns over months that no single conversation shows.

## Hard Conversations

The hard conversation you postpone gets harder: the performance problem, the behavior that's hurting the team, the promotion that isn't coming this cycle. Waiting feels kind and isn't; it denies the person the chance to fix it, and it guarantees they'll be surprised later.

Say the hard thing early, plainly and privately, with specifics (the same situation, behavior and impact), followed by what good looks like and how you'll support getting there. Then follow up, in writing, so there's no ambiguity about what was said.
